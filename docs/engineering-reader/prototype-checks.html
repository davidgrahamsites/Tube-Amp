Design sequence and physical checks

The user's proposed order is sound: prove the circuit, select parts, place parts, then route. Two corrections are required: use realistic limits before accepting a circuit, and check large-part space before final part selection.

Work order

  1. Define electrical limits, functional connections and the cabinet envelope.
  2. Check the circuit concept with models suitable for each question; ideal parts are allowed for concept checks only.
  3. Select exact available parts. Check ratings, tolerances, losses, temperature, startup and declared faults. Use validated models or documented bounds; a missing model is not a pass.
  4. Freeze each stable circuit block and its connections. Place exact parts, sockets, connectors, supports and wire routes; repeat the space check.
  5. Route with stated current, voltage, return-path, noise and heat constraints. Short connections and local bypass placement must influence placement before routing.
  6. Check the native design and prototype files. Build and measure a protected prototype, correct failures, then release production files.

Use short feedback loops between steps 2–4. Do not route an unsettled block merely to show progress. Do not wait for a production prototype to perform useful calculations or preliminary fit work. Existing drafts remain references; preserve the functional blocks and verified work.

These tests occur at different stages. They do not all have to pass before prototype PCB design. The through-chassis objective still requires its stated component, circuit, harness and fit evidence; physical commissioning remains a later gate in the existing task queue.

What must be bought now?

Nothing is ordered by this plan. The user already has the cabinet, tank, drill and DT9205A meter; that meter's exact condition and measurement ratings have not been qualified for this build. An audio interface can support low-level work, but cannot replace the high-voltage test equipment. Borrow specialist equipment or use a qualified workshop if that costs less than owning it.

Start with unpowered measurements and a mock-up. Use a protected test assembly for electrical work, then the first assembled PCB revision. No second complete amplifier is required. The prototype may become the finished amplifier after corrections and retesting.

Limits and source basis

Simulations can predict these behaviors, but cannot prove the actual assembly. Analog Devices explains the missing physical effects in MT-100. This is the basis for a measured prototype; it does not impose that document's high-speed-specific implementation advice on this tube amp.

REW's impedance procedure describes the soundcard and sense-resistor method. REW states a range up to a few hundred ohms. The seller's nominal 600 Ω input and 2,250 Ω output impedance ratings exceed that stated range; actual impedance varies with frequency and needs measurement. Do not assume its standard loudspeaker jig qualifies this tank. Use a suitable LCR meter or impedance analyzer, or validate a separate fixture against known impedances over the required range. Signal, current and input-protection limits remain to be defined.

Tektronix probe guidance requires suitable probe ratings and intact equipment protective grounds. Use appropriately rated differential measurement equipment where both measured points are above earth potential; never defeat a scope's protective earth.

Hioki protective-continuity guidance and insulation-resistance guidance distinguish electrical safety checks. Test levels and methods for this amplifier remain to be specified; no unrelated instrument standard is adopted here.

Vocabulary key

Terms used in this report. See the full key for related terms.

AC
Alternating current; current that changes direction.
Bias
A steady voltage or current that sets a device operating point.
Chassis
The metal structure that supports and encloses the electronics.
Envelope
The space occupied by a part or assembly, including stated allowances.
Feedback
A signal returned from an output to an earlier stage to control gain or response.
Harness
A grouped set of wires, contacts and connectors.
Heater
The tube element that heats the cathode.
Impedance
Opposition to alternating current, including resistance and frequency-dependent effects; measured in ohms (Ω).
Load
The circuit or device that takes current from a source.
Mains
The building AC power supply.
PCB
Printed circuit board.
Populated
With the components installed.
Reservoir
A capacitor bank that stores energy after rectification.
Tank
The mechanical spring-reverb assembly.
Transformer
A magnetic device that transfers energy between windings.
Winding
A coil of insulated wire in a transformer or inductor.
Gate
A checkpoint that requires specified evidence before the next activity.
Prototype
A test assembly used to check the design before release.
Dummy load
A resistor assembly that absorbs amplifier output power in place of a speaker during electrical tests.
LCR meter
An instrument that measures inductance, capacitance and resistance at specified test frequencies.
Sense resistor
A known resistor used to calculate current from its measured voltage drop.
Oscilloscope
An instrument that displays voltage against time.
Attenuator
A circuit that reduces a signal to a specified level.
Clipping
Signal flattening when the amplifier reaches its output limit.
Thermal
Related to heat or temperature.
Frequency response
How gain and phase change with signal frequency.
