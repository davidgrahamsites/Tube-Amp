PCB LAYOUT MUST-REMEMBERS
Design Imperatives
========================

THE CENTRAL RULE: Lay out complete current loops—not just connections between pins. The return path is part of every signal and power connection.


1. SET THE PHYSICAL CONSTRAINTS FIRST
------------------------------------

1. Start with the enclosure. Establish the board outline, mounting holes, connector positions, component heights, and cable access before routing.

2. Allow space around components. Include connector plugs, screwdrivers, heatsinks, and insertion/removal clearance—not just the component body. [1]

3. Check every footprint against the actual part. Verify pad numbering, lead pitch, finished-hole size, body dimensions, polarity, and top-view versus bottom-view drawings.

4. Never trust a footprint merely because its name matches. This is especially important for downloaded or AI-generated libraries, connectors, transistors, and tube sockets. [2]

5. Choose the manufacturer’s capabilities before setting design rules. Check trace/space limits, hole sizes, annular rings, copper thickness, solder mask, and board-edge clearances. [3]

6. Choose the layer stack deliberately. Give critical signals a nearby, continuous reference plane; do not select layers solely according to routing convenience. [4]


2. PLACEMENT COMES BEFORE ROUTING
--------------------------------

7. Group components by function. Keep each amplifier stage, regulator, interface, and associated support components together.

8. Separate noise sources from sensitive circuits. Switching power supplies and high-current paths should not occupy the same area as low-level analog inputs. [5]

9. Place the most layout-sensitive components first. Examples include switching-power components, oscillator parts, feedback networks, and bypass capacitors.

10. Follow the component manufacturer’s recommended layout. A correct schematic does not make an arbitrary placement electrically equivalent. [6]

11. Put electrostatic-discharge protection at the connector. Route the incoming signal through the protection location before it reaches the vulnerable circuit.

12. Give protection devices a short discharge-return path. A nearby protection diode with a long, inductive ground connection can still provide poor protection. [7]


3. GROUNDING AND RETURN PATHS
----------------------------

13. Ground is not an ideal zero-voltage surface. Shared return impedance lets one circuit’s current disturb another circuit’s reference. [5]

14. Prefer a continuous ground plane within each non-isolated domain. Separate noisy and quiet functions through placement and current-path control—not automatic plane splitting.

15. Do not route fast signals across ground-plane gaps. The return current must detour, increasing loop area and interference.

16. Check return paths whenever a signal changes layers. When it changes between ground reference planes, provide a nearby stitching via connecting those planes.

17. Keep heavy and pulsed returns out of sensitive reference paths. Rectifier, speaker, and power-stage currents should not share a narrow return section with an input stage.

18. Use star or bus grounding deliberately—not universally. These can suit audio power systems, but “everything must connect to one distant star” is not a universal PCB rule. [8]


4. DECOUPLING AND POWER DISTRIBUTION
-----------------------------------

19. Place bypass capacitors electrically close to their supply pins. Physical proximity is insufficient when the connecting traces take a long detour.

20. Minimize the complete bypass loop. Consider the capacitor, supply pin, device return, and ground connection together.

21. Keep bypass connections short and low-inductance. Avoid long, thin traces between the capacitor and the pins it serves.

22. Do not expect one distant bulk capacitor to replace local bypassing. Bulk energy storage and high-frequency decoupling serve different needs. [5]

23. Follow the device’s specified capacitor placement and connections. Particular regulators and isolated supplies can require capacitors directly between designated supply-and-return pin pairs. [9]

24. Avoid unnecessary shared bottlenecks in power distribution. Separate noisy branches and provide their local decoupling rather than feeding everything through one restrictive path. [10]


5. SIGNAL ROUTING
-----------------

25. Route critical nets before ordinary control signals. Preserve the geometry needed by clocks, feedback, sensitive inputs, and controlled-impedance connections.

26. Keep sensitive traces short and away from aggressors. Long parallel runs increase opportunities for coupling.

27. Judge digital routing by edge speed—not merely clock frequency. A slowly repeating signal can still have very fast transitions.

28. Calculate controlled impedance using the actual stack-up. Trace width alone does not determine impedance.

29. Route differential pairs consistently. Maintain the required width, spacing, reference continuity, and reasonably symmetric transitions.

30. Match lengths only to an actual timing requirement. Avoid adding unnecessary meanders or stubs merely to make traces look equal. [4]

31. Keep amplifier feedback networks compact. Unintended capacitance at sensitive input nodes can alter stability; copper beneath those nodes may need a deliberate keepout. [11]


6. HIGH-CURRENT AND SWITCHING-POWER LAYOUT
----------------------------------------

32. Size traces for current, copper thickness, temperature rise, and voltage drop. There is no universally correct “power trace width.”

33. Check vias as well as traces. A wide trace feeding a single small via can still create an electrical and thermal bottleneck. [12]

34. Identify the switching hot loop. Minimize the loop carrying rapidly changing current; its location depends on the converter topology.

35. Keep switching-node copper controlled. Use enough for current and heat, but do not turn a rapidly switching node into an unnecessarily large copper area.

36. Keep gate-drive loops compact. Route the drive and its return together; do not consider only the gate trace.

37. Keep feedback and sensing away from switching nodes. Short routing is not an improvement when it passes through a noisy region. [10]

38. Use Kelvin connections for current sensing. Run separate sense traces from the intended shunt measurement points, rather than measuring through current-carrying copper. [13]


7. HEAT AND COMPONENT RELIABILITY
--------------------------------

39. Design the heat-removal path, not just the electrical connection. Power parts may depend on copper area and thermal vias to reach their intended performance.

40. Treat thermal reliefs as a trade-off. They improve solderability but can restrict current and heat flow; do not apply them indiscriminately to power connections. [10]

41. Check exposed-pad electrical connections. Follow the exact datasheet; exposed metal and thermal structures are not automatically interchangeable with ground.

42. Respect package-specific keepouts. Some packages contain exposed structures that must not contact PCB copper. [6]

43. Keep precision analog circuits away from thermal gradients. Uneven heating can create measurement errors even when the electrical routing looks correct. [14]


8. HIGH VOLTAGE AND ISOLATION
----------------------------

44. Distinguish clearance from creepage. Clearance is the shortest distance through air; creepage is the shortest path along an insulating surface.

45. Do not choose safety spacing from nominal voltage alone. Consider working voltage, transients, insulation category, pollution degree, material, altitude, and the applicable equipment standard. [15]

46. Check the whole assembled insulation boundary. Include pads, solder, component leads, connectors, mounting hardware, and nearby accessible metal—not just trace spacing.

47. Do not assume a board slot solves every spacing problem. A slot can increase PCB creepage without increasing air clearance or the creepage across a component.

48. Do not casually credit solder mask or coating as safety insulation. Any claimed reduction in required spacing needs an appropriately qualified insulation/coating system. [16]

49. Keep protective earth separate from noise-control decisions. For a Class I metal-chassis build, use a secure dedicated chassis bond; never remove protective earth to cure hum. [8]


9. ASSEMBLY, IDENTIFICATION, AND TESTING
--------------------------------------

50. Check solder-mask openings and the mask between adjacent pads. Copper clearances alone do not guarantee a manufacturable solder-mask pattern.

51. Specify via treatment deliberately. Tented, open, filled, and capped vias are not equivalent manufacturing instructions. [17]

52. Make polarity and pin 1 unmistakable. Ensure markings remain useful when the part is installed.

53. Keep identification readable and off soldering surfaces. Reference designators and connector labels should not disappear beneath components or overlap exposed pads. [2]

54. Provide accessible test points on important nets. Include supplies, returns, and diagnostic signals; allow room for the actual probe.

55. Plan test access around the assembled board. Tall components, heatsinks, and enclosure hardware must not block the points you need to reach. [18]


10. FINAL KICAD CHECKS
---------------------

56. Set meaningful net classes and custom rules. High-voltage, high-current, ordinary signal, and controlled-impedance nets should not all inherit unsuitable defaults.

57. Refill every copper zone before checking the board. Inspect for disconnected islands, narrow connections, and unintentionally interrupted return paths.

58. Run design-rule checking and schematic-to-PCB parity checking. Resolve unintended shorts, unconnected items, clearance violations, and schematic mismatches.

59. Review exclusions instead of hiding problems. Every waived violation should have an understood, documented reason.

60. Inspect the exported manufacturing files. Check copper, solder mask, drill data, board outline, and layer selection—not just the editor view.

61. Treat a clean DRC as necessary, not sufficient. It checks configured rules; it does not establish analog performance, adequate cooling, or product safety. [19]


11. EXTRA REMINDERS FOR AUDIO/TUBE-AMP BOARDS
--------------------------------------------

62. Keep high-gain inputs away from later-stage outputs. Unintended coupling can create feedback, oscillation, or unwanted interaction between stages. [5]

63. Keep the connection after a grid-stopper resistor extremely short. Place it at or very near the tube’s grid connection rather than leaving a long unprotected grid lead. [20]

64. Route heater supply and return together. Use paired PCB routing and tightly twisted off-board AC heater wiring; keep them away from sensitive signal wiring.

65. Keep rectifier charging pulses local. Arrange the transformer-secondary, rectifier, and first reservoir-capacitor circuit so its pulsed current does not travel through preamp returns.

66. Plan grounding across the whole amplifier. Include jacks, pots, cable shields, board interconnects, and chassis connections; PCB grounding alone cannot control paths created elsewhere. [8]
