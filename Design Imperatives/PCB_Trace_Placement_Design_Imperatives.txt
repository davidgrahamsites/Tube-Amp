PCB TRACE-PLACEMENT DESIGN IMPERATIVES
Audio, power distribution, parasitics, RFI, and tube-amplifier layout
=================================================================

CORE PRINCIPLE

The most important rule: design the complete current loop, not just the
outgoing trace. A short signal trace with a long, indirect return path can
still produce noise and interference.

Two clarifications:

All traces have resistance, capacitance, and inductance. The goal is to
control these effects, not eliminate them.

Two unrelated traces running together create unwanted coupling or
crosstalk, not an accidental differential interface. Intentional
differential routing can be beneficial. [1]


1. COMPONENT PLACEMENT AND BOARD ORGANIZATION
-------------------------------------------

1. Place components in signal-flow order. Arrange input, gain stages,
   processing, and output so the amplified signal does not loop back
   beside the sensitive input.

2. Separate quiet and noisy regions. Keep low-level audio away from
   rectifiers, power outputs, switching regulators, relay drivers, and
   digital clocks.

3. Put the input stage near its connector. Avoid carrying an unamplified,
   high-impedance signal across the board before it reaches its first
   amplifier or buffer. [2]

4. Keep sensitive traces away from inductors and transformers on every
   layer. Routing underneath a magnetic component on the opposite side
   of the board does not automatically protect the signal. [3]

5. Place feedback components and bypass capacitors before routing general
   connections. Their positions determine whether the critical local
   loops can actually be short. [4]


2. GROUNDING AND RETURN-CURRENT PATHS
-----------------------------------

6. Prefer a continuous ground reference within each non-isolated circuit
   domain. Separate analog and digital functions primarily through
   placement and controlled current paths, not arbitrary cuts in the plane.

7. Do not route a signal across a break in its reference plane. Slots,
   cutouts, and fragmented pours force return current around the obstruction.

8. Do not cut up ground merely to eliminate every visible "loop." A
   continuous plane is not equivalent to an unwanted ground loop between
   pieces of equipment.

9. Provide a nearby return connection when a critical signal changes
   layers. Where both reference planes are the same ground, a nearby
   ground-stitching via keeps the return from taking a long detour.
   Never use this to bridge an isolation barrier. [5]

10. Do not assume return current always flows directly underneath the
    trace. At low frequencies it spreads according to resistance; at
    higher frequencies inductance increasingly favors a nearby return
    path. [5]

11. Prevent shared-impedance coupling. Speaker, rectifier, heater, relay,
    and digital currents must not develop voltage drops in copper used
    as the reference for a sensitive input. [6]

12. Use star or bus grounding where appropriate, but avoid long RF return
    "spokes." A useful low-frequency grounding arrangement can still
    have excessive inductance at radio frequencies. [6]

13. Inspect filled copper, not just ground labels. Look for narrow necks,
    disconnected islands, and long detours. A large-looking pour can still
    contain a poor current path. [7]


3. AUDIO SIGNALS, CROSSTALK, AND DIFFERENTIAL ROUTING
--------------------------------------------------

14. Route high-impedance nodes first and keep them short. Examples include
    guitar inputs, tube grids, op-amp summing nodes, and high-value
    potentiometer wipers.

15. Keep large-signal outputs away from small-signal inputs. Output-to-input
    coupling can become unintended feedback, not merely audible channel
    leakage.

16. Avoid long parallel runs between unrelated signals. Reducing the
    shared parallel length often helps more than making a small increase
    in spacing. [1]

17. Check broadside coupling between layers. Two traces directly overlapping
    on adjacent layers can couple capacitively even though they look well
    separated in a single-layer view.

18. Do not treat fabrication clearance as noise clearance. A gap that
    prevents manufacturing shorts does not necessarily provide acceptable
    crosstalk performance. [1]

19. Do not treat "three trace widths apart" as a universal guarantee.
    Coupling also depends on parallel length, trace-to-plane distance,
    signal characteristics, and the receiving circuit. [8]

20. For intentional balanced audio, route the two legs together and
    symmetrically. Match their loading and RF-filter impedances; unequal
    filtering can convert common-mode interference into differential
    noise. [9]

21. Do not add serpentine length matching to ordinary analog audio without
    a demonstrated need. Extra routing creates additional parasitics;
    transmission-line timing becomes important when propagation delay
    is significant relative to signal transitions. [10]


4. AVOIDING UNWANTED CAPACITANCE AND INDUCTANCE
--------------------------------------------

22. Minimize loop area, not just conductor length. Keep outgoing and
    returning currents close together. A straight trace with a distant
    return can be worse than a slightly longer, tightly coupled route. [11]

23. Avoid unnecessarily large copper areas on sensitive nodes. Wide traces
    and oversized pads reduce resistance but can increase capacitive
    loading. "Wider is better" is not a universal audio rule. [1]

24. Keep feedback and summing-node connections compact. Place the relevant
    resistors close to the amplifier pins, especially the inverting input;
    avoid unnecessary vias and branches there. [4]

25. Use local ground-plane exclusions only when justified. Some
    capacitance-sensitive amplifier nodes benefit from a small copper
    keepout. That is not a reason to remove the reference plane beneath
    the entire audio path. [4]

26. Do not add arbitrary shunt capacitors to "kill RF." A capacitor directly
    on an op-amp's inverting node can destabilize it; use a filter topology
    appropriate to that circuit. [9]

27. Distinguish shielding from guarding. A grounded shield intercepts
    electric-field interference. A precision guard ring controls surface
    leakage by operating near the sensitive node's voltage through a
    low-impedance connection. [12]

28. Do not let trace cosmetics outrank electrical geometry. Straightforward
    bends are sensible, but shortening critical loops and preserving
    returns matter more than making an audio board resemble a microwave
    layout. Apply controlled-impedance techniques where signal speed
    actually requires them. [10]


5. POWER DISTRIBUTION AND HIGH-CURRENT TRACES
-------------------------------------------

29. Size power traces for current, temperature rise, and voltage drop.
    Include copper thickness, route length, and thermal conditions; avoid
    simplistic rules such as "one millimeter per amp." [13]

30. Check every bottleneck. A wide power pour can still feed through a
    narrow pad connection, thermal-relief spoke, or undersized layer
    transition. [14]

31. Route each supply with its actual return path. Do not assume that
    positive and negative supply rails are automatically each other's
    return; examine the load's current paths. [15]

32. Keep high-current supply paths short and low resistance. Otherwise,
    changing load current becomes changing supply voltage and can
    modulate the audio. [2]

33. Branch sensitive and noisy loads deliberately. Do not feed a quiet
    preamp through copper that also carries large output-stage or
    relay-current changes. [6]

34. Contain the transformer-rectifier-reservoir charging loop. Connect the
    amplifier's supply and return at the intended reservoir/distribution
    nodes, not partway along a rectifier-current path. [16]

35. Use Kelvin sensing for low-level measurements of high-current circuits.
    Run separate sense connections to the intended resistor or terminal
    rather than measuring through current-carrying copper. [7]


6. DECOUPLING-CAPACITOR PLACEMENT
-------------------------------

36. Minimize the complete bypass loop. Consider the capacitor, supply pin,
    device current path, and return connection, not merely the distance
    from capacitor to supply pin.

37. Keep high-frequency bypass connections short and low inductance. A
    capacitor connected through long traces cannot provide the same RF
    bypassing as one connected locally.

38. Provide appropriate local storage as well as high-frequency bypassing.
    A distant bulk capacitor does not replace local decoupling at the
    active circuitry. [15]

39. Decouple each required supply pin or rail according to the device
    documentation. Do not assume one capacitor anywhere on a shared
    supply serves every device equally well. [15]

40. Check capacitor impedance over frequency, not just capacitance. Above
    self-resonance, a capacitor behaves inductively. Simply adding a larger
    capacitor does not guarantee better microwave-frequency suppression. [17]


7. SWITCHING REGULATORS, RELAYS, AND DIGITAL CONTROL
--------------------------------------------------

41. Design for the fastest edges, not just the repetition frequency. A
    slow control signal can still have fast transitions containing
    substantial high-frequency energy. [10]

42. Identify and minimize the switching regulator's "hot loop." This is
    the loop whose current changes abruptly between switching states;
    it is not necessarily the entire power path through the inductor. [3]

43. Keep switch-node copper compact while meeting current and thermal
    requirements. Do not extend it into a large pour beneath audio,
    feedback, or sensing circuitry. [14]

44. Keep relay suppression local. Place the flyback diode or specified
    clamp close to the coil circuit, and keep the associated
    switching-current path out of sensitive audio references. [18]

45. Treat ferrite beads as designed filters, not magic fixes. Check
    impedance at the interference frequency and actual DC current;
    bead-and-capacitor combinations can create unwanted resonance. [19]

46. Treat class-D outputs as switching-power nets. Follow the amplifier's
    recommended output-filter and return layout rather than routing them
    like ordinary line-level audio. [20]


8. RFI FILTERING, CABLE ENTRY, AND SHIELDING
-----------------------------------------

47. Stop RF before it reaches a nonlinear input stage. An amplifier need
    not amplify a radio carrier normally to rectify it into offsets or
    unwanted low-frequency interference. Filtering afterward cannot undo
    the detection. [9]

48. Put cable-entry filtering at the entry point. Do not let an unfiltered
    external conductor travel through the quiet region before reaching
    its filter. Use a short, appropriate RF return connection. [21]

49. Keep the dirty and clean sides of a filter physically separated.
    Nearby traces or overlapping copper can capacitively bypass the
    filter you just added. [12]

50. Use short, broad shield-to-chassis connections. Long pigtails add
    inductance. Enclosure seams, openings, and cable penetrations are
    part of the shielding system, not separate mechanical details. [21]

51. For balanced connections, terminate the cable shield to chassis at the
    connector entry. Do not route shield current through the quiet
    audio-reference copper: the classic "pin 1" problem. [22]

52. Do not apply "ground every shield at one end" universally. Balanced
    shields and unbalanced signal returns have different jobs; an
    unbalanced cable's shield commonly carries the signal return itself. [22]

53. Do not expect a thin copper pour to solve 50/60 Hz magnetic hum. First
    reduce loop area, increase distance, and improve transformer/wiring
    orientation. Low-frequency magnetic shielding may require
    high-permeability material. [11]


9. ADDITIONAL PRIORITIES FOR YOUR TUBE AMPLIFIER
----------------------------------------------

54. Put grid-stopper resistors at the tube socket's grid connection. Keep
    the connection between stopper and grid extremely short; a long trace
    after the stopper remains exposed to interference. [23]

55. Keep plate/anode wiring away from grids and early-stage inputs. Apply
    this across layers and between the two sections of a dual-triode
    socket. Large voltage swings make unintended coupling particularly
    undesirable. [16]

56. Route heater supply and return together. Keep the pair away from input
    and reverb-recovery circuitry; use tightly paired routing on the PCB
    and twisted pairs for appropriate off-board wiring. [16]

57. Associate each stage's return with its supply-decoupling node. Keep
    local stage currents separate from the currents replenishing the
    supply capacitors. [16]

58. Treat the spring-reverb return as a sensitive input and the send as a
    potential aggressor. Keep their routes separated rather than bundling
    them together through the amplifier. This follows the same
    low-level/high-level separation rule as the main input and output. [2]

59. Keep footswitch-control wiring out of the sensitive audio region.
    Place switching circuitry to keep audio connections short while
    containing coil and driver currents locally. [2]


10. MAINS AND HIGH-VOLTAGE LAYOUT
-------------------------------

60. Treat insulation boundaries as mandatory exceptions to
    continuous-ground advice. Never extend a ground plane, guard trace,
    or stitching via across a required isolation barrier to improve
    EMC. [24]

61. Set spacing from actual insulation requirements, not nominal voltage
    alone. Consider working voltage, transients, insulation grade,
    material group, pollution conditions, and altitude.

62. Check clearance and creepage separately. Clearance is the shortest
    air distance; creepage is the shortest path along an insulating
    surface.

63. Review the assembled geometry. Pads, leads, mounting hardware, board
    edges, slots, and adjacent components can determine the shortest
    insulation path, not just trace-to-trace spacing.

64. Do not assume a slot or solder mask automatically makes a layout safe.
    Slots must meet the relevant dimensional rules; insulation credit
    requires the applicable construction and qualification. [25]


11. TURN THE GUIDELINES INTO KICAD CHECKS
---------------------------------------

65. Create meaningful net classes. Suggested categories include AUDIO_HIZ,
    AUDIO_LOWZ, POWER_HIGH_CURRENT, SWITCHING, HEATER, B_PLUS, and MAINS.
    These are organizational categories, not instructions to split all
    their grounds.

66. Separate manufacturing rules from electrical requirements. Use
    appropriate widths and clearances, plus custom rules and keepouts
    where needed. KiCad 10 supports creepage constraints as well as
    ordinary clearance checks.

67. Refill zones and inspect all layers before sign-off. Check minimum
    connection widths, disconnected copper, sensitive routes beneath
    noisy components, and clearances around real footprints. [26]

68. Do not equate a clean DRC report with a quiet amplifier. DRC checks
    configured geometric and connectivity rules; prototype testing is
    still needed to reveal coupling, ringing, instability, and noise. [26]


PRIORITY ORDER
--------------

Safe insulation boundaries
    -> Deliberate return paths
    -> Compact high-current loops
    -> Short sensitive nodes
    -> Separation of noisy and quiet circuitry
    -> Effective filtering and shielding
