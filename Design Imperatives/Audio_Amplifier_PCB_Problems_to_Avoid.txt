AUDIO AMPLIFIER PCB PROBLEMS TO AVOID
A design-review reference for solid-state and tube amplifiers.


1. GROUNDING, HUM, AND BUZZ
=========================

1. Sharing input-ground paths with speaker current.
   Keep high-current output returns out of the input and feedback reference
   paths; shared impedance can create distortion or oscillation.
   Reference: https://www.ti.com/lit/ds/symlink/lm3886.pdf

2. Large rectifier-reservoir charging loops.
   Keep the transformer secondary, rectifier, and first reservoir capacitor
   connections compact and separate from quiet audio returns.

3. Accidental ground connections through hardware.
   Check jack sleeves, potentiometer bodies, mounting holes, shields, and metal
   standoffs for unintended circuit-ground-to-chassis connections.
   Reference: https://www.valvewizard.co.uk/Grounding.pdf

4. Treating every "GND" point as electrically identical.
   Trace resistance and inductance matter, especially where large or rapidly
   changing currents flow.

5. Assuming "star ground" or "ground plane" automatically solves everything.
   Choose the grounding arrangement around actual return-current paths; do not
   split planes or join grounds indiscriminately.
   Reference: https://sound-au.com/earthing.htm


2. SIGNAL ROUTING AND INTERFERENCE
==================================

6. Putting the output stage beside the input stage.
   Arrange the board so large output signals do not couple back into sensitive
   inputs.

7. Long high-impedance input traces.
   Keep input wiring and traces short, with the input stage and its associated
   components nearby.

8. Long parallel runs between sensitive and noisy signals.
   Avoid running input traces alongside outputs, supply switching nodes, or
   high-level signals -- including on adjacent layers.
   Reference: https://www.ti.com/lit/ds/symlink/lm3886.pdf

9. Poorly routed balanced inputs.
   Route the two signal conductors together and maintain similar surroundings
   rather than sending them through different parts of the board.
   Reference: https://www.ti.com/lit/an/sboa237/sboa237.pdf

10. Placing interference sources beside sensitive circuitry.
    Separate transformers, switching supplies, and high-current switching
    sections from input and low-level recovery stages; include the off-board
    wiring in this review.
    Reference: https://www.valvewizard.co.uk/Grounding.pdf


3. FEEDBACK, STABILITY, AND OUTPUT CONNECTIONS
=============================================

11. Large feedback loops.
    Place feedback resistors and compensation components close to the amplifier
    pins.

12. Excess copper around sensitive feedback nodes.
    Extra pad area, vias, and nearby planes add capacitance. Follow the device's
    layout guidance rather than automatically pouring ground everywhere.
    Reference: https://www.analog.com/en/resources/analog-dialogue/articles/high-speed-printed-circuit-board-layout.html

13. Ignoring minimum stable gain.
    An amplifier may oscillate when used below its specified stable gain, even
    though the schematic otherwise looks reasonable.

14. Omitting or relocating output-stability components.
    Implement the required compensation, output isolation, or resistor-capacitor
    network for the actual amplifier and load -- not a copied universal
    arrangement.
    Reference: https://www.ti.com/lit/ds/symlink/lm3886.pdf

15. Grounding a bridge-tied speaker output.
    In a bridge-tied-load amplifier, neither speaker terminal is necessarily
    ground. Do not join channel "negative" outputs together unless the design
    explicitly permits it.
    Reference: https://www.ti.com/lit/ug/slou020a/slou020a.pdf


4. POWER DISTRIBUTION AND DECOUPLING
===================================

16. Using large reservoir capacitors instead of local bypass capacitors.
    The main capacitor bank does not replace the bypassing required at each
    amplifier.

17. Correct bypass values but poor placement.
    Minimize the complete capacitor-device-return loop, not just the distance
    from the capacitor to the supply pin.
    Reference: https://www.analog.com/en/resources/analog-dialogue/articles/high-speed-printed-circuit-board-layout.html

18. Allowing stages to interact through the supply.
    Provide the intended local filtering and return connections so changing
    current in one stage does not modulate another.
    Reference: https://www.valvewizard.co.uk/Grounding.pdf

19. Wide power traces with hidden narrow bottlenecks.
    Check pad exits, vias, connectors, copper necks, and thermal-relief spokes --
    not only the main trace width.
    Reference: https://www.ti.com/lit/pdf/sloa103a

20. Rating capacitors only for nominal running voltage.
    Check the maximum voltage actually possible, including startup, high mains,
    and light-load conditions; do not exceed capacitor ratings.
    Reference: https://www.nichicon.co.jp/english/series_items/catalog_pdf/e-aluminum.pdf


5. COMPONENTS, HEAT, AND RELIABILITY
===================================

21. Reversed electrolytic capacitors.
    Verify polarity against the actual voltages. On a negative supply rail, the
    capacitor's positive terminal may connect to ground.
    Reference: https://www.nichicon.co.jp/english/series_items/catalog_pdf/e-aluminum.pdf

22. Ignoring capacitor ripple-current and temperature ratings.
    Keep capacitors away from concentrated heat and leave their pressure-relief
    vents unobstructed.
    Reference: https://www.nichicon.co.jp/english/series_items/catalog_pdf/e-aluminum.pdf

23. Inadequate thermal paths.
    Design the heatsink, exposed-pad connection, copper area, and thermal vias as
    part of the amplifier -- not as additions after routing.
    Reference: https://www.ti.com/lit/SLYT182

24. Assuming a metal tab or thermal pad is ground.
    It may be connected to a supply rail. Verify the exact package before
    attaching it to a grounded heatsink.
    Reference: https://www.ti.com/lit/ds/symlink/lm3886.pdf

25. Flux residue and contamination around high-impedance nodes.
    Specify suitable cleaning and inspect these areas carefully; surface leakage
    can upset bias and increase noise.
    Reference: https://www.analog.com/en/resources/technical-articles/signal-conditioning-for-high-impedance-sensors.html


6. CHANNEL SWITCHING, MUTING, AND FOOTSWITCH CONTROL
==================================================

26. Assuming debouncing prevents audio pops.
    Debouncing addresses repeated switching commands; DC discontinuities and
    stored charge require separate attention.

27. Leaving disconnected audio nodes floating.
    Provide appropriate DC reference or discharge paths without excessively
    loading the signal.
    Reference: https://e2e.ti.com/support/switches-multiplexers-group/switches-multiplexers/f/switches-multiplexers-forum/1114638/faq-how-can-i-reduce-the-click-and-pop-noise-on-my-audio-switch-and-detection-jack

28. Ignoring power-on and power-off transients.
    Design muting and settling behavior for both directions of the power cycle.
    Reference: https://www.analog.com/en/resources/technical-articles/how-to-pick-a-clickandpop-suppressor.html

29. Missing or unsuitable relay-coil suppression.
    Clamp the coil transient, but account for the clamp's effect on relay release
    time and the driver's voltage rating.
    Reference: https://www.te.com/en/products/relays-and-contactors/electromechanical-relays/intersection/relay-coil-suppression-dc-relays.html

30. Using power-relay contacts for tiny audio signals without checking suitability.
    Verify low-level switching performance and minimum-load guidance, not merely
    the maximum current rating.
    Reference: https://www.ia.omron.com/support/faq/answer/36/faq02810/

31. Floating logic inputs or slow edges into ordinary CMOS inputs.
    Define control states and use appropriate input conditioning; do not assume
    an unused or disconnected input is harmless.
    Reference: https://e2e.ti.com/support/logic-group/logic/f/logic-forum/737694/faq-how-does-a-slow-or-floating-input-affect-a-cmos-device


7. TUBE-AMPLIFIER-SPECIFIC BOARD PROBLEMS
=======================================

32. Long unprotected grid connections.
    Keep the connection after a grid-stopper resistor as short as practicable;
    long grid leads increase susceptibility to parasitic oscillation.
    Reference: https://www.valvewizard.co.uk/gridstopper.html

33. Large AC-heater loops near signal wiring.
    Keep heater conductors closely paired, appropriately sized, and away from
    sensitive signal paths.

34. Floating heater supplies.
    Provide the intended DC reference or elevation arrangement; a DC heater
    supply is not automatically hum-free.

35. Exceeding heater-to-cathode voltage limits.
    Check elevated-cathode stages, particularly cathode followers, against the
    selected tube's ratings.
    Reference: https://www.valvewizard.co.uk/heater.html

36. Remote stage-filter capacitors and poorly placed returns.
    Keep each stage's supply-current loop controlled; inadequate local decoupling
    can produce low-frequency "motorboating" oscillation.
    Reference: https://www.valvewizard.co.uk/Grounding.pdf


8. MAINS AND HIGH-VOLTAGE SAFETY
==============================

37. Using ordinary PCB default clearances for mains or tube high voltage.
    Determine creepage and clearance from working voltage, transients, insulation
    requirements, board material, environment, and altitude -- not one universal
    spacing.

38. Checking only visible top-layer spacing.
    Review every copper layer, connector, component body, mounting fastener, and
    chassis interface for insulation-barrier problems.

39. Treating an unqualified coating as permission to reduce spacing.
    Only claim insulation or creepage benefits supported by the applicable
    coating system and safety requirements.
    Reference: https://www.ti.com/lit/ml/slup421/slup421.pdf

40. Relying on signal-ground wiring for protective earth.
    Use a dedicated, secure chassis safety-earth connection.
    NEVER DISCONNECT PROTECTIVE EARTH TO CURE HUM.
    Reference: https://sound-au.com/earthing.htm

41. Assuming "unplugged" means safe.
    Provide a discharge and verification plan for stored energy; treat exposed
    circuitry as potentially live until its voltage has been checked.
    Reference: https://www.tek.com/en/documents/technical-brief/floating-oscilloscope-measurements-and-operator-protection


9. FOOTPRINTS, CONNECTORS, AND ASSEMBLY
======================================

42. Trusting a footprint because its name looks correct.
    Verify symbol pin -> footprint pad -> physical component terminal.

43. Confusing top and bottom views.
    Check orientation particularly carefully for tube sockets, relays,
    transistors, and connectors.

44. Incorrect pitch, hole size, or body clearance.
    Compare footprints against the exact purchased part's drawing.

45. Unclear assembly markings.
    Make pin 1, polarity, connector functions, and supply labels unambiguous.

46. Mounting hardware colliding with components or copper.
    Include screws, washers, standoffs, and enclosure clearances in the mechanical
    review.

47. Poor solderability around large copper areas.
    Review thermal reliefs, mask openings, paste requirements, and exposed-pad
    assembly instructions.
    Reference: https://docs.kicad.org/10.0/en/pcbnew/pcbnew.html


10. VERIFICATION BEFORE ORDERING AND ENERGIZING
==============================================

48. A PCB that no longer matches the schematic.
    Update it from the schematic and run schematic-parity checks.

49. Treating a clean design-rule check as proof of a good amplifier.
    Configure the correct rules and review every exception; automated checks do
    not establish noise performance or stability.

50. Not inspecting the fabrication outputs.
    Review the generated copper, solder-mask, board-outline, and drill files --
    not just the editable PCB view.
    Reference: https://docs.kicad.org/10.0/en/pcbnew/pcbnew.html

51. Assuming no audible squeal means no oscillation.
    Parasitic oscillation may be ultrasonic; include suitable instrument checks
    in validation.
    Reference: https://www.valvewizard.co.uk/gridstopper.html

52. Unsafe oscilloscope grounding.
    A conventional scope's ground clip is normally earth-referenced. Use
    appropriately rated differential or isolated measurement equipment where
    required; never defeat the scope's protective earth.
    Reference: https://www.tek.com/en/documents/technical-brief/floating-oscilloscope-measurements-and-operator-protection
