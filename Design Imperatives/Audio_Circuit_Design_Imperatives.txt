AUDIO CIRCUIT DESIGN MUST-REMEMBERS
=================================

For every stage, know its DC bias, signal level, input and output impedance,
noise contribution, bandwidth, and current-return path.


1. SIGNAL LEVELS, GAIN, AND HEADROOM
----------------------------------

1. Define nominal and maximum signal levels—not just “audio level.” Specify
   the source, load, and units: volts RMS, volts peak, volts peak-to-peak,
   dBu, or dBV. These are not interchangeable.
   Source: https://www.ap.com/fileadmin-ap/technical-library/How_to_write_and_read_audio_specs.pdf

2. Budget headroom at every stage. Include maximum input, gain settings,
   equalizer boosts, and summed signals. A clean final output stage cannot
   undo accidental clipping earlier in the circuit.

3. Decide where distortion is intentional. In a guitar amp, clipping may be
   the objective; unintended clipping in a buffer, effects return, or reverb
   recovery stage is a separate design problem.
   Source: https://valvewizard.co.uk/Book1.html

4. Calculate loading with everything connected. Volume controls, tone
   networks, reverb sends, parallel branches, and the following stage all
   affect the circuit—not just the component labeled “load.”
   Source: https://www.analog.com/en/resources/technical-articles/using-DS1802-pushbutton-digital-potentiometer-create-audio-preamplifier-attenuator.html

5. Usually bridge impedances rather than match them. For voltage-signal
   connections, the receiving input should generally have substantially
   higher impedance than the driving output. Transformer and power-stage
   load requirements are different.
   Source: https://www.ranecommercial.com/legacy/note109.html


2. DC BIAS AND COUPLING CAPACITORS
--------------------------------

6. Every high-impedance input needs a defined DC return path. A coupling
   capacitor does not provide one. Check this with cables unplugged and
   switches in every position.
   Source: https://www.analog.com/en/resources/app-notes/an-937.html

7. Treat a single-supply midpoint reference as a real circuit. It needs
   adequate filtering, suitable impedance, and enough current capability
   for its loads. A noisy reference can bypass the amplifier’s otherwise
   good supply-noise rejection.
   Source: https://www.analog.com/en/resources/analog-dialogue/articles/common-problems-when-designing-amplifier-circuits.html

8. Calculate coupling-capacitor cutoff frequencies. For a simple one-pole
   RC section:

       f_c = 1 / (2 × π × R_seen × C)

   Resistance is in ohms, capacitance in farads, and frequency in hertz.
   Include the actual surrounding impedances.
   Source: https://www.analog.com/en/resources/technical-articles/using-DS1802-pushbutton-digital-potentiometer-create-audio-preamplifier-attenuator.html

9. Avoid unnecessary DC across volume-control tracks and switching
   contacts. Changing a connection between different DC potentials creates
   an unwanted voltage step; coupling and bias arrangements must account
   for this.
   Source: https://www.analog.com/en/resources/technical-articles/using-DS1802-pushbutton-digital-potentiometer-create-audio-preamplifier-attenuator.html

10. Choose capacitors by electrical behavior, not “audio-grade” labeling.
    Check voltage rating, polarity, leakage, and capacitance stability.
    Film or C0G/NP0 types are useful for low-distortion signal filtering
    where practical; not all ceramic dielectrics behave alike.
    Source: https://www.ti.com/video/4650746830001


3. NOISE AND COMPONENT CHOICES
-----------------------------

11. Choose the input device for the source impedance. Low voltage noise
    alone does not guarantee a quiet circuit: input-current noise flowing
    through source impedance also produces noise voltage.

12. Do not make every resistor enormous—or tiny. Larger resistances can
    increase noise; smaller resistances increase loading and required drive
    current. Select values for the complete circuit.

13. Protect the first low-level stages especially carefully. Noise
    introduced there is amplified by subsequent stages. Improving a later
    stage will not remove noise already mixed with the signal.
    Source: https://e2e.ti.com/support/amplifiers-group/amplifiers/f/amplifiers-forum/1126121/faq-understanding-the-parameters-for-audio-op-amps

14. Specify noise over a bandwidth. A noise-density figure in nV/√Hz is not
    total RMS noise. Compare measurements using the same bandwidth and
    weighting.
    Source: https://e2e.ti.com/support/amplifiers-group/amplifiers/f/amplifiers-forum/1126121/faq-understanding-the-parameters-for-audio-op-amps

15. Stop radio-frequency interference before it reaches a nonlinear input
    stage. Otherwise, an amplifier can demodulate radio-frequency energy
    into audible interference. Input filtering must also preserve the
    wanted audio response.
    Source: https://www.valvewizard.co.uk/gridstopper.html


4. AMPLIFIER LIMITS AND STABILITY
--------------------------------

16. Check noise gain as well as signal gain. Op-amp stability depends on
    the feedback network and its frequency response—not merely the
    advertised audio gain. Confirm any minimum stable-gain requirement.
    Source: https://e2e.ti.com/blogs_/archives/b/precisionhub/posts/resistors-in-the-feedback-of-a-buffer-ask-why

17. Check input common-mode range and loaded output swing. “The supply
    rails are high enough” does not prove the amplifier can accept or
    produce the required waveform.
    Source: https://e2e.ti.com/cfs-file/__key/communityserver-discussions-components-files/14/CircuitCookbook_2D00_OpAmps.pdf

18. Check slew rate at the largest required signal. A sine wave requires
    a maximum slope of:

        2 × π × f × V_peak

    Use peak voltage, keep the units consistent, and allow margin rather
    than designing exactly at the limit.
    Source: https://edgeworker.ti.com/lit/pdf/tidu765

19. Treat cables as capacitive loads. A circuit that works into a resistor
    may ring or oscillate when connected to a long cable. Use the
    manufacturer’s recommended isolation or compensation approach.

20. Do not assume a faster op-amp is a drop-in upgrade. Recheck feedback
    components, layout, decoupling, load capacitance, and stability whenever
    the device changes.
    Source: https://www.analog.com/en/resources/analog-dialogue/articles/techniques-to-avoid-instability-capacitive-loading.html


5. GROUNDING AND SHIELDING
-------------------------

21. Draw the return-current paths, not just the signal wires. “Ground” is
    not an ideal zero-impedance connection; current through shared
    resistance and inductance creates unwanted voltages.

22. Keep noisy return currents out of sensitive input references. Speaker,
    rectifier-charging, relay, and digital currents should not share
    avoidable impedance with low-level audio returns.
    Source: https://www.analog.com/en/resources/technical-articles/successful-pcb-grounding-with-mixedsignal-chips--follow-the-path-of-least-impedance.html

23. Do not blindly apply “star ground everything” or “split every ground
    plane.” Choose the arrangement from the actual current paths. A
    continuous plane with sensible component placement is often preferable
    to arbitrary splits.
    Source: https://www.analog.com/en/resources/analog-dialogue/articles/what-are-the-basic-guidelines-for-layout-design-of-mixed-signal-pcbs.html

24. Distinguish cable shielding from signal return. Balanced-interface
    shields generally belong at the chassis entry; an unbalanced cable’s
    shield commonly also carries signal-return current. “Ground the shield
    at only one end” is not a universal rule.

25. Never disconnect protective earth to cure hum. Correct the signal
    interconnection, shield termination, or isolation arrangement instead.
    Source: https://www.ranecommercial.com/legacy/note110.html


6. PHYSICAL LAYOUT AND POWER CLEANLINESS
--------------------------------------

26. Keep sensitive nodes and feedback paths physically compact. Wiring and
    PCB traces add capacitance and inductance; these become part of the
    circuit whether drawn on the schematic or not.
    Source: https://www.analog.com/en/resources/analog-dialogue/articles/techniques-to-avoid-instability-capacitive-loading.html

27. Separate low-level inputs from high-level outputs and fast switching
    circuitry. Avoid routing them together where coupling can create
    crosstalk or unintended feedback.
    Source: https://www.analog.com/en/resources/technical-articles/successful-pcb-grounding-with-mixedsignal-chips--follow-the-path-of-least-impedance.html

28. Put supply decoupling close to the device it serves. A capacitor near
    the regulator does not replace local decoupling at an amplifier,
    converter, or switching device. Keep the associated current loops short.
    Source: https://www.analog.com/en/resources/analog-dialogue/articles/what-are-the-basic-guidelines-for-layout-design-of-mixed-signal-pcbs.html

29. Design supply and reference filtering separately. A clean supply does
    not automatically make a resistor-derived bias reference clean;
    reference noise can enter the signal directly.
    Source: https://www.analog.com/en/resources/analog-dialogue/articles/common-problems-when-designing-amplifier-circuits.html

30. Consider magnetic pickup as well as electrical shielding. Transformer
    spacing and orientation matter, especially around spring-reverb
    hardware. A shielded cable does not solve every magnetic-coupling
    problem.
    Source: https://www.valvewizard.co.uk/reverbdriver.html


7. CONTROLS, SWITCHING, AND FOOTSWITCHES
--------------------------------------

31. Debouncing and pop suppression are different jobs. Debouncing prevents
    multiple control events. It does not eliminate DC steps or
    discontinuities in the audio waveform.
    Source: https://www.ti.com/document-viewer/lit/html/SCEA094

32. Do not leave disconnected audio nodes floating. Provide appropriate
    bias or discharge paths so stored charge does not produce a pop when
    the connection returns.

33. Design the audio transition deliberately. Consider DC equalization,
    break-before-make switching, and a controlled mute or ramp where
    necessary. A mechanically perfect switch can still make an audible
    click.
    Source: https://e2e.ti.com/support/switches-multiplexers-group/switches-multiplexers/f/switches-multiplexers-forum/1114638/faq-how-can-i-reduce-the-click-and-pop-noise-on-my-audio-switch-and-detection-jack

34. For your footswitch, keep the switching circuitry near the audio
    circuit. Use the pedal cable for touch-safe low-voltage control, rather
    than sending sensitive tube-stage audio out to the pedal. Make startup,
    unplugged-cable, and fault states explicit design requirements.

35. Suppress relay-coil transients and keep their currents local. The coil
    stores energy that must go somewhere when switched off; select a clamp
    compatible with the driver and required release behavior.
    Source: https://www.ti.com/document-viewer/lit/html/SNVAA45


8. MIXING, CONNECTORS, AND OUTPUTS
--------------------------------

36. Never directly tie ordinary amplifier outputs together to mix them.
    Use a suitable resistor network or summing amplifier. Splitting one
    output into suitable loads is not the same as combining multiple
    outputs.
    Source: https://www.ranecommercial.com/legacy/note109.html

37. Track polarity and allow headroom at summing points. Two identical,
    in-phase signals added at unity gain produce twice the voltage—
    approximately +6 dB. Opposite polarity can cancel shared signal content.
    Source: https://ez.analog.com/dsp/sigmadsp/f/q-a/564774/what-are-the-possible-options-for-summing-left-and-right-signal-into-mono-signal

38. Balanced does not necessarily mean equal-and-opposite output voltages.
    Impedance balance is fundamental to interference rejection; a correctly
    implemented impedance-balanced output can drive only one signal
    conductor.
    Source: https://www.sounddevices.com/output-balancing-topologies/

39. Document every connector pin explicitly. A TRS connector could carry
    balanced mono, unbalanced stereo, an insert send/return, or footswitch
    control. Its shape does not define its function.
    Source: https://www.ranecommercial.com/legacy/pdf/ranenotes/Grounding_%26_Shielding_of_Audio_Devices.pdf

40. Identify the output topology before connecting a load or probe. In
    particular, neither speaker terminal of a bridge-tied-load amplifier
    should be assumed to be ground. An oscilloscope ground clip can short
    an active output.
    Source: https://www.ti.com/mx/lit/pdf/slou346


9. EXTRA REMINDERS FOR YOUR TUBE AMP AND SPRING REVERB
---------------------------------------------------

41. A grid stopper and a grid-leak resistor do different jobs. The grid
    leak establishes the DC reference; the stopper helps control
    radio-frequency pickup, parasitic oscillation, and grid current. One
    does not replace the other.
    Source: https://www.analog.com/en/resources/app-notes/an-937.html

42. Check overload recovery, not just the clipping waveform. Grid current
    can shift bias and cause blocking distortion. Bass response, coupling
    time constants, and grid-current limiting must be considered together.
    Source: https://valvewizard.co.uk/Book1.html

43. Match the reverb driver to the tank electrically. Check drive current,
    impedance versus frequency, transformer requirements, and component
    stresses. A tank’s nominal impedance is not its DC resistance.

44. Protect against an unplugged reverb tank. A transformer-driven tank is
    an inductive-load problem as well as an audio problem; review flyback,
    damping, and connector behavior. Keep magnetic hum pickup in mind when
    positioning the transformer.
    Source: https://www.valvewizard.co.uk/reverbdriver.html

45. Define the wet/dry interface before choosing final values. For your
    build, explicitly review send loading, recovery gain, mix level,
    polarity, and whether switching reverb off should preserve or cut the
    remaining tail.


10. VERIFICATION BEFORE CALLING IT FINISHED
-----------------------------------------

46. Test more than a 1 kHz sine wave. Measure response and distortion across
    the intended frequency range and at meaningful signal levels and loads.
    Source: https://www.ap.com/fileadmin-ap/technical-library/How_to_write_and_read_audio_specs.pdf

47. Record the conditions beside every performance result. Include gain
    setting, source and load impedance, input/output level, measurement
    bandwidth, and weighting. An isolated “THD” or “noise” number is
    incomplete.
    Source: https://www.ap.com/fileadmin-ap/technical-library/How_to_write_and_read_audio_specs.pdf

48. Check stability with realistic cables and loads. Look for overshoot,
    ringing, and oscillation—not just an audible squeal. A pleasant
    listening test is not a stability test.
    Source: https://www.analog.com/en/resources/analog-dialogue/articles/techniques-to-avoid-instability-capacitive-loading.html

49. Make a state-by-state test checklist. Cover startup, shutdown, mute,
    bypass, channel changes, reverb changes, unplugged connectors, and
    control extremes. Test switching both during silence and while passing
    audio.
    Source: https://e2e.ti.com/support/switches-multiplexers-group/switches-multiplexers/f/switches-multiplexers-forum/1114638/faq-how-can-i-reduce-the-click-and-pop-noise-on-my-audio-switch-and-detection-jack

50. Treat test equipment as part of the circuit—and the safety review.
    Confirm voltage ratings and earth connections before probing. Never
    defeat an oscilloscope’s protective earth; use appropriately rated
    differential or isolated measurement equipment where required.
    Source: https://www.tek.com/en/documents/technical-brief/floating-oscilloscope-measurements-and-operator-protection


FINAL REVIEW QUESTION
=====================

Does this circuit still behave correctly at maximum signal, minimum load
impedance, every control setting, and every switching state—not merely
under its easiest operating conditions?
