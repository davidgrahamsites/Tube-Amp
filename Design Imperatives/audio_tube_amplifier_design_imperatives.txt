AUDIO TUBE AMPLIFIER DESIGN — MUST-REMEMBERS
==========================================

For conventional transformer-coupled tube amplifiers, including guitar amps and hi-fi amps. B+ means the high-voltage DC supply; plate and anode mean the same thing.

SAFETY: Tube amplifiers contain potentially lethal voltages. Stored capacitor energy can remain after unplugging; an isolation transformer does not make the internal high-voltage supply safe to touch. Have the mains and high-voltage construction reviewed by someone qualified before energizing it.

Reference: https://www.valvewizard.co.uk/


1. DEFINE THE AMPLIFIER BEFORE CHOOSING COMPONENTS
------------------------------------------------

1. Specify the intended behavior: clean hi-fi reproduction, clean guitar headroom, deliberate overdrive, or a combination. Do not automatically treat a desirable guitar distortion characteristic as good hi-fi performance.

2. Define output power together with load, frequency range, and distortion limit. A wattage figure alone is an incomplete design target.

3. Choose the output tubes, operating voltages, bias, and output transformer together. They are interdependent, not independent shopping decisions.

4. Distinguish topology from operating class. Push-pull does not automatically mean Class AB, and cathode bias does not automatically mean Class A.

Reference: https://www.valvewizard.co.uk/pp.html


2. HIGH-VOLTAGE AND MAINS SAFETY
------------------------------

5. Use a mains-isolated power supply. For your build, specify 220 V, 50 Hz input compatibility for every mains-powered supply, including any separate control supply.

6. Give protective earth a dedicated, mechanically secure chassis connection. Never switch, fuse, or disconnect protective earth to cure hum. Audio grounding is a separate design problem.

Reference: https://www.valvewizard.co.uk/Grounding.pdf

7. Specify fuses properly: current, time characteristic, voltage, breaking capacity, and location. Check whether secondary faults can bypass the protection; never casually copy a 120 V amplifier’s fuse value.

8. Provide discharge paths for stored high voltage. Check every capacitor bank and switch position. A bleeder resistor or extinguished indicator is not proof that the circuit is discharged—verify with appropriately rated equipment.

Reference: https://www.valvewizard.co.uk/fuses.html

9. Rate switches for the actual circuit they interrupt. A mains AC rating does not automatically establish suitability for several hundred volts DC.

10. Determine insulation spacing from actual requirements. Working voltage, transients, insulation category, board material, contamination, and altitude matter; there is no universal “tube-amp clearance” number.

Reference: https://www.valvewizard.co.uk/standby.html


3. TUBE RATINGS AND OPERATING POINTS
----------------------------------

11. Use the exact tube manufacturer’s datasheet. Verify pinout, heater requirements, voltage limits, dissipation, grid-circuit resistance, and heater-to-cathode limits.

12. Do not assume tubes sharing a socket are interchangeable. Even common nine-pin dual triodes can have different heater connections.

Reference: https://www.jj-electronic.com/images/stories/product/preamplifying_tubes/pdf/ecc83s.pdf

13. Calculate idle plate dissipation using plate-to-cathode voltage:

    P_plate,idle = (V_plate - V_cathode) × I_plate

    Use volts and amperes to obtain watts.

14. Check dissipation throughout the output range—not only at idle or full power. In Class AB, the worst operating point can occur between them.

15. Do not use “70% bias” as a universal design rule. Safe bias depends on operating voltage, reflected load, tube characteristics, and operating class.

16. Treat screen-grid dissipation separately. Safe plate dissipation does not guarantee safe screens, particularly during overdrive. Size screen resistors for current limiting, heat, and fault-voltage stress.

Reference: https://valvewizard.co.uk/gridstopper.html


4. BIAS AND GRID PROTECTION
--------------------------

17. Give every control grid a defined DC return path. Check normal operation, switching transitions, disconnected controls, and broken potentiometer wipers.

18. Respect the maximum grid-circuit resistance. Include the relevant grid stopper, grid-leak resistor, and bias-source resistance—not merely the resistor labelled “grid leak.”

Reference: https://aikenamps.com/index.php/grid-resistors-why-are-they-used

19. Make adjustable fixed bias fail toward a colder operating point. Limit the adjustment range and address loss of wiper contact.

20. Check bias during startup, shutdown, and rapid power cycling. Power tubes must not remain conducting with substantial B+ but inadequate negative bias.

21. Do not treat cathode bias as automatic protection. Verify resistor dissipation, bypass-capacitor stress, current sharing, and behavior under sustained drive.

Reference: https://www.valvewizard.co.uk/bias.html


5. OUTPUT TRANSFORMER AND SPEAKER LOAD
--------------------------------------

22. Match reflected primary impedance to the operating point. The transformer reflects the connected speaker load:

    Z_primary = (N_primary / N_secondary)^2 × Z_load

23. Do not confuse winding DC resistance with rated audio impedance. A resistance-meter reading is not the transformer’s specified primary load.

24. Specify bass performance at the required output power. A transformer that handles its rated watts at guitar frequencies may not handle the same power at deep hi-fi bass frequencies.

Reference: https://www.aikenamps.com/index.php/output-transformers-explained

25. Use a transformer suitable for the DC operating conditions. Conventional single-ended stages need DC-current capability and an appropriate core gap; push-pull stages require attention to current imbalance.

Reference: https://www.hammfg.com/electronics/transformers/audio/overview

26. Account for plate swings above B+. Insulation and component ratings must accommodate signal peaks and inductive transients. Do not drive the amplifier without its intended load unless unloaded operation has been specifically protected and validated.

Reference: https://www.aikenamps.com/index.php/idle-current-biasing-why-70-percent


6. SIGNAL PATH, GAIN, AND OVERLOAD
---------------------------------

27. Budget gain and headroom stage by stage. Decide which stage should clip first; include losses from tone controls, mixers, effects loops, and feedback.

28. Account for loading between stages. Tone controls and volume pots affect the preceding stage’s gain, frequency response, and available signal swing.

29. Calculate coupling-capacitor behavior using the actual surrounding circuit. Larger capacitors are not automatically better; excessively low-frequency response can be undesirable.

Reference: https://valvewizard.co.uk/Common_Gain_Stage.pdf

30. Control blocking distortion. Grid current can charge coupling capacitors and temporarily shift bias; assess recovery after hard clipping, not just the clipped waveform.

31. Put grid stoppers close to the tube’s grid pin. Long wiring after the resistor undermines parasitic-oscillation suppression. Choose values for stability, overload behavior, and bandwidth together.

Reference: https://aikenamps.com/index.php/grid-resistors-why-are-they-used

32. Give the phase inverter adequate loaded output swing. Balance alone is insufficient: check drive capability and clipping behavior with the actual power-tube grid networks attached.

Reference: https://www.valvewizard.co.uk/acltp.html


7. B+ SUPPLY AND FILTERING
-------------------------

33. Design for high mains and light-load/startup voltage. B+ can rise before the tubes conduct; normal warm operating voltage is not the worst-case capacitor voltage.

34. Do not equate transformer AC current with available DC current. Capacitor-input rectifiers draw charging pulses that increase winding RMS current and heating.

Reference: https://valvewizard.co.uk/psu.html

35. Match reservoir capacitance to the rectifier and transformer. More capacitance increases charging stress; tube rectifiers have specific reservoir-capacitance and source-resistance requirements.

36. Give sensitive stages suitable supply decoupling. Filtering must suppress ripple and prevent stages from coupling through their shared supply.

37. For series electrolytics, check voltage sharing and reduced capacitance. Design balancing resistors from capacitor leakage and operating conditions, and verify their voltage and power ratings.

Reference: https://www.valvewizard.co.uk/smoothing.html

38. Do not add standby circuitry by habit. Define its purpose, switching stress, discharge behavior, and effect on bias. A standby switch is not a substitute for correctly rated components.

Reference: https://www.valvewizard.co.uk/standby.html


8. HEATER SUPPLIES
-----------------

39. Check heater voltage at the sockets under load, across the intended mains range—not only at the transformer terminals.

40. Budget all heater current, including added reverb tubes and indicators. Rectified DC heater supplies require extra transformer-current allowance.

41. Give the heater supply one deliberate reference scheme. Avoid floating heaters and conflicting references on opposite sides of a rectifier.

42. Check heater-to-cathode voltage for every stage, including startup. Cathode followers and elevated-cathode circuits may require heater elevation or separate windings.

43. Keep heater current loops small. Twist AC heater pairs and separate them from sensitive signal wiring. DC heaters must actually be clean; poorly filtered DC can introduce noise.

Reference: https://www.valvewizard.co.uk/heater.html


9. GROUNDING AND PHYSICAL LAYOUT
-------------------------------

44. Design current-return paths—not just ground symbols. Do not let the chassis become an accidental shared signal-return conductor.

45. Keep rectifier charging currents out of input-stage returns. Place the rectifier–reservoir loop compactly and route its return deliberately.

46. Ground each stage with its associated local supply-decoupling return. Prevent output-stage and control-circuit currents from sharing sensitive input paths.

47. Separate low-level inputs from large-signal outputs. Short grid wiring and sensible stage placement help prevent unintended feedback.

48. Plan chassis connections at jacks, pots, shields, and mounting hardware. Accidental additional connections can defeat an otherwise sound grounding scheme.

Reference: https://www.valvewizard.co.uk/Grounding.pdf


10. FEEDBACK AND STABILITY
-------------------------

49. Verify feedback polarity with the actual transformer connections. Incorrect phasing can turn intended negative feedback into positive feedback.

50. Validate stability beyond the audible band. An amplifier can sound normal while suffering ultrasonic oscillation.

51. Test feedback with realistic reactive loads and control extremes. Presence, resonance, feedback switches, and output-tap changes can alter loop behavior.

52. Do not assume more feedback is always better. It changes gain, distortion, output impedance, clipping behavior, and stability requirements.

Reference: https://www.aikenamps.com/index.php/what-is-negative-feedback


11. COMPONENTS, CONSTRUCTION, AND VALIDATION
-------------------------------------------

53. Check voltage ratings as well as wattage. Screen resistors, plate resistors, and supply-dropping resistors may face startup or fault stresses far beyond their normal dissipation.

54. Design for the temperature inside the finished enclosure. Keep heat-sensitive components away from tubes and hot resistors; validate after the amplifier reaches operating temperature.

Reference: https://aikenamps.com/index.php/technical-q-a

55. Verify every symbol-to-footprint pin mapping against the physical part. Include tube-socket viewing direction, transformer leads, switched jacks, and relay contact states.

56. Never defeat oscilloscope protective earth. Ordinary bench-scope ground clips are generally earth-referenced; use properly rated differential or isolated measurement equipment where required.

Reference: https://www.tek.com/en/documents/technical-brief/floating-oscilloscope-measurements-and-operator-protection

57. Test before relying on listening impressions. Use a suitably rated resistive dummy load for power measurements, then validate reactive-load stability, clipping recovery, hum, temperature, bias drift, and startup/shutdown behavior. Record expected voltages and test conditions.

Reference: https://aikenamps.com/index.php/technical-q-a


12. YOUR SPRING REVERB AND FOOTSWITCH ADDITIONS
---------------------------------------------

58. Match the reverb tank, driver, and transformer as a system. Tank impedance is frequency-dependent; the driver needs adequate current, not merely voltage gain.

59. Protect the reverb driver against an unplugged tank and flyback transients. Keep its transformer away from magnetic hum sources and its low-level recovery wiring away from the driver.

Reference: https://www.valvewizard.co.uk/reverbdriver.html

60. Keep the external footswitch interface touch-safe and low voltage. Specify relay isolation and switching ratings; separately address debounce, audio-pop suppression, coil transients, startup defaults, and TRS insertion shorts. Debouncing alone does not make audio switching quiet.

Reference: https://www.ia.omron.com/product/cautions/36/safety_precautions.html
